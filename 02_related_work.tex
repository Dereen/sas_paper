Flipper control ranges from manual teleoperation to three families of autonomy:
geometry-based planning, hierarchical switching, and reinforcement learning. We summarize
each, note the representative works we reimplement as baselines
(Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}), and then draw out the assumption they share. We then turn
to the one line of work that controls flippers without a map, and show that it addresses
a different task. Table~\ref{tab:sensing} summarizes what each method must sense.

\subsection{Manual and mode-based teleoperation}
The baseline for everything below is direct teleoperation: the operator drives the
flippers by hand~\cite{cihala2025}, or by switching between preset flipper
modes~\cite{larochelle2013,azayev2022}. This needs no map, since the operator observes the terrain and
closes the control loop by hand, and with an expert operator it remains the
traversal-quality ceiling, but it demands the operator's continuous
attention~\cite{cihala2025}.

\subsection{Geometry-based / model-based planning}
A family that plans flipper motion from an explicit terrain model, and is the
\emph{most} map-dependent of all. Chen et al. combine dynamic programming with a
fast geometry-based pose predictor over the elevation
point cloud~\cite{chen2023}; Yuan et al. plan in the flipper configuration space
from known stair geometry with motion-capture ground truth~\cite{yuan2019}; Oehler
and von Stryk predict pose on a signed-distance field built from
LiDAR~\cite{oehler2024}; and Xu et al. solve a receding-horizon hybrid trajectory
optimization over a simplified terrain sampled from a 3D occupancy map with FAST-LIO
odometry~\cite{xu2024}; and, most recently, Zhang et al. plan the path and flipper
maneuvers with RRT* over a contact-model pose predictor, using learned semantic
segmentation of the elevation map only as a perception front-end~\cite{zhang2025}. Both
Chen and Xu drive the left and right flippers as a symmetric pair, and Xu's model is planar outright, so they are confined to terrain with little lateral variation and cannot handle asymmetric obstacles, where the two sides must act differently.

\subsection{Hierarchical / switching / mixture-of-experts control}
A family that composes low-level behaviors under a selector. The adaptive-traversability line~\cite{zimmermann2014} classifies
terrain into discrete flipper modes from an elevation map, and for the selected mode a
simple low-level controller drives the flippers. Azayev and Zimmermann~\cite{azayev2022}
learn a state machine that switches among seven hand-crafted flipper templates. Both the
state selection and the per-flipper observations are drawn from an elevation map built
from an Ouster LiDAR with ICP-based localization~\cite{pomerleau2013icp}, so the map
decides which template runs as well as how it is parameterized.
The map is the one maintained by the CTU robot deployment system~\cite{roucek2022subt}.
Crucially for us, each state overlays a \emph{roll}-stabilization term and a
stagnation-triggered \emph{escape maneuver}, the origin of the anti-stuck recovery we
adopt, which lowers both the front and the rear flippers to free the robot when it stalls.
It is thus the one prior method that couples anti-stuck recovery with attitude
stabilization, but it stabilizes roll only and overlays the two without the
directional-priority blend we add to keep them from fighting
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method-blend}).

\subsection{Monolithic model-free deep RL}
This family trains one end-to-end policy to map sensing directly to flipper commands.
FTR-Bench standardizes this setting, benchmarking SAC, PPO, TD3, DDPG, and TRPO on a
flipper-track robot in Isaac Lab~\cite{ftrbench2025}. Pecka et al. learn a
safety-constrained policy (Constrained REPS) whose state is body pitch plus the terrain
height read from an online octomap~\cite{pecka2016}. Discrete-action variants dominate the
flipper-specific literature: Pan et al. learn a dueling double-DQN over discrete
front/rear flipper increments~\cite{pan2023atd3qn} and extend it with an
intrinsic-curiosity module~\cite{pan2023icm}; both take a local elevation map,
downsampled from a LiDAR point-cloud map built by \mbox{LiDAR} odometry and mapping
(ALOAM), plus chassis pitch, and shape only the pitch for smoothness; roll is
uncontrolled and ``stuck'' enters only as a terminal penalty. Mitriakov et al.
learn staircase negotiation with a policy-gradient method, first from step-edge
geometry with marker-based ground truth~\cite{mitriakov2021} and then in an
open-source Gazebo/ROS framework from depth-image features with ground-truth
pose~\cite{mitriakov2022}, injecting stability through center-of-gravity and
pitch-rate reward shaping. Most recently, C-TRAC learns a contact-aware latent from noisy
LiDAR and drives an asymmetric-SAC policy that, unusually, stabilizes both roll and pitch
via a normalized-energy-stability-margin reward~\cite{ctrac2025}. All of these consume an
elevation map, or a geometric surrogate of one, together with an estimated robot pose.
The dependency does not weaken over the period surveyed: the more recent methods enlarge
the perceptual input rather than reduce it, so the accuracy of the map built by LiDAR
odometry bounds their performance at least as tightly as it bounds the earlier ones.

\subsection{Attitude stabilization and mapless posture control}\label{sec:related-posture}
Stabilizing body attitude with flippers is not new, but it has almost always been done
on top of a map. Azayev and Zimmermann add a roll-only stabilization term inside their
map-based state machine~\cite{azayev2022}; C-TRAC learns to level both roll and pitch,
but from a reward computed on the map-based simulator state~\cite{ctrac2025}. The
exception is Rocha et al.~\cite{rocha2023}, who regulate roll, pitch, and chassis height
of an articulated tracked vehicle from the IMU and the flipper joints alone. The
controller treats the four flipper anchor points as the legs of a Stewart platform,
maps a desired chassis motion to flipper joint velocities through differential
kinematics, keeps every flipper on the ground by watching the sign of its joint torque,
and estimates ground clearance as the distance from the chassis to the plane through
the four flipper contact points. It needs no exteroceptive sensor and no map, and it is
the closest prior work to ours in what it senses.

It is not, however, an obstacle-crossing controller, and does not claim to be one. Its
authors state the operating envelope explicitly: the method \emph{``assumes
non-structured ground with the absence of major disruptions (e.g., steps, gaps,
vertical obstacles) and slopes with inclinations up to \ang{30}''}~\cite{rocha2023}.
That is the complement of our setting: steps, gaps, and vertical obstacles are the only
terrain on our course. The map-free line of work stops where obstacle crossing begins, and
\SAS{} carries it across that boundary by adding recovery to stabilization.

\subsection{Evaluating traversal quality}
We evaluate a controller by the quality of the traversal it produces, measured
on the robot's body. The quantities used for this are physical rather than
perceptual: mainly IMU-based smoothness such as pitch swing and vertical
shock~\cite{chen2023}, body attitude, and chassis-to-ground clearance.
Following~\cite{cihala2025,zimmermann2014} we score shock, tilt, and clearance,
and we compute all three against ground truth. In simulation the reference
comes from the simulator; on the real robot, from external localization and a
handcrafted map of each obstacle.
