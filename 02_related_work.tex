% ============================ RELATED WORK ============================
% Structure: manual + three autonomy families (reactive/geometry-based, hierarchical, RL).
We group the flipper controllers into manual control and three families of autonomous
control: reactive and geometry-based, imitation learning, and reinforcement learning.
Table~\ref{tab:sensing} summarizes the compared controllers and what they
require.

\subsection{Manual control}
In direct teleoperation, the operator drives the flippers by hand~\cite{cihala2025}, or
switches between preset flipper modes~\cite{larochelle2013,azayev2022}. Manual control
needs no map: the operator observes the terrain from a third-person view, closes the control
loop by hand, and also stabilizes the robot.

\subsection{Reactive and geometry-based control}
These controllers are the most dependent on an explicit terrain model because they compute the flipper commands either
reactively~\cite{cihala2025} or by planning. Chen et al. combine dynamic programming with a
fast geometry-based pose predictor over the elevation
point cloud~\cite{chen2023}; Yuan et al. plan in the flipper configuration space
from known stair geometry with motion-capture ground truth~\cite{yuan2019}; Oehler
and von Stryk predict pose on a signed-distance field built from
LiDAR~\cite{oehler2024}; Xu et al. solve a receding-horizon hybrid trajectory
optimization over a simplified terrain sampled from a prebuilt 3D occupancy map with
FAST-LIO2 odometry~\cite{xu2024}; and most recently, Zhang et al. plan the path and flipper
maneuvers with RRT* over a contact-model pose predictor, using learned semantic
segmentation of the elevation map only as a perception front-end~\cite{zhang2025}.

\subsection{Imitation learning}
These controllers learn from demonstrations which low-level behavior to apply. The
adaptive-traversability line~\cite{zimmermann2014} classifies
terrain into discrete flipper modes from an elevation map. 
A learned state machine~\cite{azayev2022}
switches among seven hand-crafted flipper morphologies selected from an elevation map. In addition, each
state overlays a \emph{roll}-stabilization term and a stagnation-triggered
\emph{anti-stuck} term that lowers all four flippers to free the robot~\cite{azayev2022}.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning}
Pecka et al. learn a
safety-constrained policy (Constrained REPS) whose state is the body pitch and the terrain
height \SI{20}{\centi\metre} ahead of the robot, observed from an occupancy map built online from the LiDAR scans~\cite{pecka2016}. Discrete-action variants dominate the
flipper-specific literature: Pan et al. learn a dueling double-DQN over discrete
front/rear flipper increments~\cite{pan2023atd3qn} and extend it with an
intrinsic-curiosity module~\cite{pan2023icm}; both take the chassis pitch and the average
terrain heights of a few segments ahead of, below, and behind the robot. Their reward favors smooth pitch only, and getting stuck is a terminal
penalty. Mitriakov et al.
learn staircase negotiation with a policy-gradient method from step-edge geometry~\cite{mitriakov2021}, injecting stability through center-of-gravity and
pitch-rate reward shaping. Most recently, C-TRAC learns a contact-aware latent representation from noisy LiDAR and
drives an asymmetric-SAC policy that stabilizes both roll and pitch via a
normalized-energy-stability-margin reward~\cite{ctrac2025}. We train our RL baselines in
FTR-Bench~\cite{ftrbench2025}, an Isaac Lab framework that benchmarks standard RL
algorithms on a flipper robot.

\subsection{Attitude stabilization and mapless posture control}\label{sec:related-posture}
Flipper-based attitude stabilization has been studied before, but nearly always on top of
an elevation map: a roll-only stabilization term inside a map-based state
machine~\cite{azayev2022}, and C-TRAC stabilizing both roll and pitch from a reward computed
on the map-based simulator state~\cite{ctrac2025}. Rocha et al.~\cite{rocha2023} regulate
roll, pitch, and ground clearance of an articulated tracked vehicle from the IMU and the
flipper joints.

\subsection{Evaluating traversal quality}
To evaluate traversal quality, multiple metrics were proposed: IMU-based smoothness
measured on the robot's body, such as pitch swing and vertical
shock~\cite{chen2023}, body attitude, and chassis-to-ground clearance read from the
ground-truth map~\cite{cihala2025,zimmermann2014}.
