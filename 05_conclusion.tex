% ============================ CONCLUSION ============================
State-of-the-art flipper controllers depend on an elevation map of the occluded ground under
the robot chassis, which drifts with odometry on exactly the terrain where flippers are needed.
The comparison shows which part of a traversal the choice of controller actually
governs. Shock does not separate the controllers: over all thirteen of them, it spans $0.865$ to $0.918$ in simulation and $0.765$ to $0.798$ on the real pallet. Every controller
transmits a similar acceleration to the body, set by the platform and the terrain rather
than by the flipper motion.
What separates them is how they stabilize the body: clearance and
tilt; on the real robot, clearance is the most significant factor, which spreads from $0.301$ to $0.545$. That channel is also the one the transfer from
simulation damages most: every controller drops from $0.68$--$0.75$ in simulation to
$0.30$--$0.55$ on the real robot, while shock and
tilt keep their level. The onboard
map costs quality throughout: in simulation, every controller scores lower under it than
under the ground-truth map, by $0.008$ to $0.069$, against $0.020$ for \SAS{} through its
motion cue alone.

\SAS{} reaches the highest traversal quality of all autonomous controllers in simulation under both
maps, and on the real pallet, it is second under the onboard
map and among the leading controllers under the ground-truth map. It recovers from every stall by its own anti-stuck term. Its weakest channel is on the real pallet, where it holds a clearance of $0.363$ under the
ground-truth map and $0.419$ under the onboard map, below the best map-based controllers because,
 without a terrain preview, it can only raise the chassis once the obstacle is already
under it. 

On a real log hidden in tall grass, invisible to the LiDAR and absent from the
onboard map, the map-based controller Yuan~\cite{yuan2019} got stuck under the log while \SAS{} crossed it.
Two principles as simple as \emph{hold the body level} and \emph{dig out when motion
stops} can reach the traversal quality of the map-based state of the art.

All comparisons reset the perception stack at each obstacle, which limits the drift the
onboard map can accumulate; therefore, the reported map degradation is a lower bound, and a long,
uninterrupted traversal remains to be tested.
Future work targets a more robust motion cue by fusing LiDAR-ICP odometry with camera
odometry, which should hold up in occluded, feature-poor, or flat places where ICP alone struggles, and a fallback scheme that defers to a map-based
controller when the onboard map is trustworthy and to \SAS{} when it is not.
