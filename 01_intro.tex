% ============================ INTRODUCTION ============================
Tracked and wheeled robots cannot climb an obstacle taller than their leading wheel.
\emph{Articulated} tracked robots address higher obstacles with \emph{flippers},
independently actuated sub-tracks that reshape the robot's morphology on the fly: they
reach beyond the leading wheel to climb what a rigid chassis cannot, and they free the
robot when it gets \emph{stuck}, failing to advance despite a forward
command~\cite{cihala2025,azayev2022,xu2024}.

The \emph{six-track} design keeps a main driven track on
each side of the chassis and adds four smaller flippers for climbing and stabilizing, with
the two tracks on each side coupled to a single velocity. The \emph{four-track} design
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}) drops the main tracks and drives four larger, independently actuated
flippers alone~\cite{rocha2023}.

\textbf{Prior flipper controllers assume an elevation map.}
Across the literature,
autonomous and semi-autonomous flipper controllers share a precondition: they consume
an \emph{elevation map} of the ground under and in front of the robot. Geometric planners
and learned policies alike take that map as their dominant
input~\cite{chen2023,oehler2024,yuan2019,xu2024,pan2023icm,pan2023atd3qn,ctrac2025,azayev2022}.
Fully manual teleoperation needs no map because the operator sees the terrain, but must
drive all four flippers by hand. Mapless posture control~\cite{rocha2023} levels the
chassis from proprioception alone, but does not cross obstacles.

\textbf{On a real robot, the map is a dead-reckoning artifact.}
A flipper controller must know the geometry of the ground its tracks and flippers are about
to contact, which lies directly beneath and immediately ahead of the chassis; where that
estimate drifts, the flipper angles it commands are computed for the wrong ground. Onboard sensors
observe what is ahead, but the body occludes their view of the terrain beneath it.
Dedicated underbody sensors could recover it, but they are rarely fitted and have very
low resolution. That terrain can therefore mainly be \emph{remembered}: observed earlier, accumulated into a robot-centric elevation
map, and carried beneath the robot as it advances by
\emph{integrating wheel or LiDAR odometry}~\cite{fankhauser2018}. Every stage of this
pipeline is a dead-reckoning process: point-cloud deskewing warps each scan by the estimated
sensor trajectory, and elevation mapping registers successive scans through the
estimated motion~\cite{loam2014,pomerleau2013icp}. On the loose, deformable,
step-like terrain where flippers are needed, odometry is least reliable: tracks slip, and
the chassis pitches and drops abruptly, saturating the IMU, and inertial integration cannot
recover the estimate either~\cite{woodman2007imu}.

\textbf{The hardest case is a hidden obstacle.}
Mapping reports the surface its sensors observe, which is not in general the surface the
tracks will bear on, so a controller planning on it commands flipper angles for a surface
that will not bear the load.
When a rigid obstacle is \emph{hidden}, optical sensors---LiDAR and cameras---return the
compliant canopy above it, and the load-bearing obstacle beneath stays invisible until the
chassis stalls on it. Neither a dedicated underbody sensor, which the same vegetation
blinds, nor foliage-penetrating radar, which is bulky and low-resolution, resolves this in
practice.

\textbf{Our contribution: control of the flippers without a map and without localization.}
We present \emph{Stabilizing Anti-Stuck} (\SAS), a reactive controller that requires neither
an elevation map nor an estimated localization of the robot. It keeps the robot body
horizontal regardless of the terrain, whether it is climbing an obstacle or descending one,
and has two parts: it stabilizes the body, and it frees the robot when it gets stuck, by
driving the flippers down. It is not odometry-free, since a short-horizon motion cue tells it whether the
body is advancing, but it never accumulates pose and therefore never inherits the drift that
corrupts the map. It has no terrain preview, so it cannot anticipate an obstacle before
contact, yet it keeps operating where every map-based baseline breaks down.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\nopagebreak
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We identify and quantify a dependency shared by state-of-the-art flipper controllers: a requirement for an elevation map of the ground \emph{under} the robot, which is occluded from onboard sensing and can only be maintained by odometry integration, whose drift degrades it in the field. 
  We measure this degradation by evaluating map-based baselines under both a \emph{ground-truth} map and the \emph{onboard-estimated} map.
  \item We propose \SAS, a mapless, localization-free flipper controller combining
  continuous roll+pitch stabilization with reactive anti-stuck recovery under a
  directional-priority blend.
  \item We deploy \SAS{} on a real four-track robot (MARV) and evaluate it in simulation
  and on real obstacles against \emph{manual}
  teleoperation~\cite{larochelle2013,cihala2025} and \emph{autonomous} controllers, both
  switching between reactive experts~\cite{cihala2025,zimmermann2014} and map-based
  planners~\cite{azayev2022,chen2023,pan2023atd3qn,xu2024,ctrac2025}.
\end{enumerate}
