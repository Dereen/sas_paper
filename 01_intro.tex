% ============================ INTRODUCTION ============================
Mobile ground robots for rough terrain come in three main configurations: legged, wheeled, and tracked.
Legged robots clear the widest range of obstacles but must be actively actuated
just to stay upright, which gives them a low payload-to-power-consumption ratio; wheeled and tracked
robots stay upright passively and carry far more for the same power.
Tracked and wheeled robots can still struggle in one respect: they cannot climb
an obstacle taller than their leading wheel.
\emph{Articulated} tracked robots address this with \emph{flippers}, independently
actuated sub-tracks that reshape the robot's morphology on the fly: they add the
reach to climb tall obstacles while keeping the high payload-to-power-consumption ratio of
a tracked platform~\cite{cihala2025,azayev2022,xu2024}.

Two flipper layouts are common. The \emph{six-track} design keeps a main driven track on
each side of the chassis and adds four smaller flippers used only to climb and stabilize;
the two tracks on each side are coupled, so the left pair and the right pair each move at a
single velocity. The \emph{four-track} design (Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}) drops the main tracks
and drives four larger, independently actuated flippers alone~\cite{rocha2023}: the
independent tracks make it lighter and more maneuverable, but with nothing under the body, it
grounds out or \emph{stalls} more readily. Either way, getting \emph{stuck} and failing
to advance despite a forward command threatens any ground robot.

\textbf{Prior flipper controllers assume an elevation map.}
Flipper control spans a spectrum of operator involvement: \emph{manual} teleoperation
of all four flippers, \emph{semi-autonomous} controllers the operator supervises or
switches between, and \emph{fully autonomous} control that manages the flippers
without intervention~\cite{cihala2025}. Higher autonomy asks less of the
operator but shifts the burden onto onboard perception. Across the literature,
autonomous and semi-autonomous flipper controllers share a precondition: they consume
an \emph{elevation map} of the ground under and in front of the robot. Geometric methods
predict the robot's pose over a discretized flipper configuration space on an elevation
map or point cloud~\cite{chen2023,oehler2024}, or plan flipper motions through
configuration space search and trajectory optimization over an explicit terrain
model~\cite{yuan2019,xu2024}. Learned
policies~\cite{pan2023icm,pan2023atd3qn,ctrac2025,azayev2022} take the elevation map
as the dominant part of their observation. Two things stand outside this pattern, and
neither crosses obstacles autonomously. Fully manual teleoperation needs no map because
the operator sees the terrain, but must drive all four flippers by hand. Mapless posture
control~\cite{rocha2023} levels the chassis from proprioception alone, but its authors
restrict it to ground without steps, gaps, or vertical obstacles
(Sec.~\ref{sec:related-posture}), which is exactly the terrain in question.

\textbf{On a real robot, the map is a dead-reckoning artifact.}
The terrain that matters most to a flipper controller is directly under and just ahead of
the chassis. Onboard sensors observe what is ahead, but the body occludes their view of the
terrain beneath it.
Dedicated underbody sensors could recover it, but they are rarely fitted and have very
low resolution. That terrain can therefore mainly be \emph{remembered}: observed earlier, accumulated into a robot-centric elevation
map, and carried beneath the robot as it advances by
\emph{integrating wheel or LiDAR odometry}~\cite{fankhauser2018}. Every stage of this
pipeline is a dead-reckoning process: point-cloud deskewing warps each scan by the estimated
sensor trajectory, and elevation mapping registers successive scans through the
estimated motion~\cite{loam2014,pomerleau2013icp}. On the loose, deformable,
step-like terrain where flippers are needed, odometry is least reliable: tracks slip, and
the chassis pitches and drops abruptly, saturating the IMU. Inertial integration does not
recover the estimate either. Double integration turns a constant accelerometer bias into a
position error growing as $t^{2}$, while a gyroscope bias tilts the estimated gravity
direction, leaking a component of gravity into the horizontal axes and producing a position
error growing as $t^{3}$~\cite{woodman2007imu}.

\textbf{The hardest case is a hidden obstacle.}
Elevation mapping reports the surface its sensors observe, which is not in general the
surface the tracks will bear on.
When a rigid obstacle is \emph{hidden}, optical sensors---LiDAR and cameras---return the
compliant canopy above it, and the load-bearing obstacle beneath stays invisible until the
chassis stalls on it. A dedicated underbody sensor does not help here either: the same
vegetation is pressed against the body and blinds it. Longer-wavelength sensors such as
foliage-penetrating radar can see through vegetation, but they are bulky and
low-resolution. \TODO{citation for foliage-penetrating radar.}
A controller planning on such a map optimises a flipper trajectory that is feasible for the
observed surface and infeasible for the true one: the support it plans for rests on
vegetation that will not bear the load, and the commanded flipper angles may intersect the
occluded obstacle. Vegetation, snow, water, and loose debris occlude in the same way.

\textbf{Our contribution: control of the flippers without a map and without localization.}
Instead of hardening the perception stack against this failure, we remove the dependency: we control the flippers from onboard proprioception and a short-window motion cue, with no elevation map and no integration of odometry.
We present \emph{Stabilized Anti-Stuck} (\SAS), a controller that requires neither an elevation map nor an estimated localization of the robot.
It is not odometry-free, since a short-horizon motion cue tells it whether the body is advancing; it is nonetheless \emph{localization-free and integration-free}, because it never accumulates pose and therefore never inherits the drift that corrupts the map. 
Its dominant signal is the roll and pitch reported by the IMU, which are \emph{gravity-referenced and therefore drift-free}.
From these signals \SAS{} superimposes two per-flipper velocity terms: an attitude-stabilization term that drives body roll and pitch toward a level attitude, and a reactive anti-stuck term that, when the motion cue reports a deficit, drives the flippers down to push the robot forward again, whether or not the tracks grip the ground. 
A directional-priority rule gives stabilization precedence: the anti-stuck term may reinforce a flipper's stabilization command but never flip its sign, so a flipper that stabilization is raising is never driven downward by anti-stuck, and one being lowered is never forced up.

This is not free. \SAS{} has no terrain preview, so it cannot anticipate an obstacle before contact, and its anti-stuck term can still produce oscillatory dig-release behavior.
Its value is that it keeps the robot level and keeps operating in the regime where the map, and thus every map-based baseline, breaks down, using sensing that is always available and never drifts.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\nopagebreak
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We identify and quantify a dependency shared by state-of-the-art flipper controllers: a requirement for an elevation map of the ground \emph{under} the robot, which is occluded from onboard sensing and can only be maintained by odometry integration, whose drift degrades it in the field. 
  We measure this degradation by evaluating map-based baselines under both a \emph{ground-truth} map and the \emph{onboard-estimated} map.
  \item We propose \SAS, a mapless, localization-free flipper controller whose
  inputs are drift-free proprioception (gravity-referenced IMU attitude and the
  flipper joints) plus a short-horizon motion cue, combining continuous roll+pitch
  stabilization with
  reactive anti-stuck recovery under a directional-priority blend.
  \item We deploy \SAS{} on a real four-track robot (MARV) and evaluate it on real
  obstacles against baselines at three levels of autonomy: \emph{manual}
  teleoperation~\cite{larochelle2013,cihala2025}, \emph{semi-autonomous} switching between
  reactive experts~\cite{cihala2025,zimmermann2014}, and \emph{autonomous} map-based
  controllers~\cite{azayev2022,chen2023,pan2023atd3qn,xu2024,ctrac2025}, using success
  rate and traversal quality (acceleration/shock, tilt, and ground clearance).
  \item We give an honest account of its limitations (residual dig-release
  oscillation, and no terrain preview, so it cannot anticipate an obstacle
  before contact) and characterize the operating regime in which mapless
  control is the preferable choice.
\end{enumerate}
