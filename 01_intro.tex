% ============================ INTRODUCTION ============================
Mobile ground robots for rough terrain come in three main configurations: legged, wheeled, and tracked.
Legged robots clear the widest range of obstacles but must be actively actuated
just to stay upright, which gives them a low payload-to-power-consumption ratio; wheeled and tracked
robots stay upright passively and carry far more for the same power.
Tracked and wheeled robots can still struggle in certain conditions: they cannot climb
an obstacle taller than their leading wheel, they can tilt and tip-over on uneven ground,
and crossing an obstacle jolts the body and its payload with abrupt pitch and shock.
\emph{Articulated} tracked robots address all three problems with \emph{flippers}, independently
actuated sub-tracks that reshape the robot's morphology on the fly: they add the
reach to climb tall obstacles, help keep the body level, and cushion the transitions that
would otherwise shake it~\cite{cihala2025,azayev2022,xu2024}.

Two flipper layouts are common. The \emph{six-track} design keeps a main driven track on
each side of the robot's chassis and adds four smaller flippers used only to climb and stabilize, with the main tracks driving the robot. The \emph{four-track}
design (Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}) drops the main tracks and drives four larger flippers
alone~\cite{rocha2023}. While this configuration is lighter and more maneuverable, with nothing under the body, it
grounds out or \emph{stalls} more readily. Either way, getting \emph{stuck} and failing
to advance despite a forward command threatens any ground robot, and once the tracks lose their grip, only the flippers can free it.

\textbf{Prior flipper controllers assume an elevation map.}
Flipper control spans a spectrum of operator involvement: \emph{manual} teleoperation
of all four flippers, \emph{semi-autonomous} controllers the operator supervises or
switches between, and \emph{fully autonomous} control that manages the flippers
without intervention~\cite{cihala2025}. Higher autonomy lowers the operator's
cognitive load but shifts the burden onto onboard perception. Across the literature,
autonomous and semi-autonomous flipper controllers share a precondition: they consume
an \emph{elevation map} of the ground under and around the robot. Geometric methods
predict the robot's pose over a discretized flipper configuration space on an elevation
map or point cloud~\cite{chen2023,oehler2024}, or plan flipper motions through
configuration space search and trajectory optimization over an explicit terrain
model~\cite{yuan2019,xu2024}. Learned
policies~\cite{pan2023icm,pan2023atd3qn,ctrac2025,azayev2022} take the elevation map,
transformed into the robot frame, as the dominant part of their observation. The one
exception is fully manual teleoperation: the operator sees the terrain, so no map is
needed, but must drive all four flippers by hand~\cite{cihala2025}.

\textbf{On a real robot, the map is a dead-reckoning artifact.}
The terrain that matters most to a flipper controller is directly under and just ahead of
the chassis. Onboard sensors see what is ahead, but the body blocks their view of the
terrain beneath it, which is exactly the terrain that warns of an imminent stall.
Dedicated underbody sensors could recover it, but they are rarely fitted and can be
blinded themselves by the tall grass pressed against the body. That terrain can therefore
only be \emph{remembered}: observed earlier, accumulated into a robot-centric elevation
map, and carried beneath the robot as it advances by
\emph{integrating wheel odometry}~\cite{fankhauser2018}. Every stage of this pipeline is
a dead-reckoning process: point-cloud deskewing warps each scan by the estimated
sensor trajectory, and elevation mapping registers successive scans through the
estimated motion~\cite{loam2014,pomerleau2013icp}. On the loose, deformable,
step-like terrain where flippers are needed, odometry is least reliable: tracks
slip, the chassis pitches and drops abruptly, and the integrated pose drifts.

\textbf{The hardest case is a hidden obstacle.}
Elevation mapping reports the surface the sensor sees, not the one the tracks will touch.
When a rigid obstacle is \emph{hidden}, the optical sensors a mobile robot carries, LiDAR
and cameras, return the compliant canopy above it, and the load-bearing obstacle beneath
stays invisible until the chassis stalls on it. Longer-wavelength sensors such as
foliage-penetrating radar can see through vegetation, but they are bulky, low-resolution,
and absent from standard flipper-robot payloads. Here the map does not drift, it is simply
wrong: a controller that trusts it drives onto the hidden obstacle or moves the flippers
into an unsafe configuration. Vegetation, snow, water, and loose debris hide obstacles the
same way.

\textbf{Our contribution: control of the flippers without a map and without localization.}
Rather than pursue an ever-more-robust perception stack, we control the flippers from onboard proprioception alone, with no map and no integration. 
We present \emph{Stabilized Anti-Stuck} (\SAS), a controller that needs no elevation map and no estimate of where the robot is. 
It is not odometry-free (it uses a short-horizon motion cue to tell whether the body is moving), but it is \emph{localization-free and integration-free}: it never accumulates pose, so it never inherits the drift that corrupts the map. 
Its dominant signal is the roll and pitch reported by the IMU, which are \emph{gravity-referenced and therefore drift-free}: they are measured instantaneously against gravity, with no integration and no memory of past motion. 
From these signals \SAS{} superimposes two per-flipper velocity terms: an attitude-stabilization term that drives body roll and pitch toward a level attitude, and a reactive anti-stuck term that, when the motion cue reports a deficit, drives the flippers down to push the robot forward again, whether or not the tracks grip the ground. 
A directional-priority rule gives stabilization precedence: the anti-stuck term may reinforce a flipper's stabilization command but never flip its sign, so a flipper that stabilization is raising is never driven downward by anti-stuck, and one being lowered is never forced up.

This comes at a cost. \SAS{} does not attain the peak traversal quality of a well-fed map-based controller, and its anti-stuck term can still produce oscillatory dig-release behavior. 
Its value lies elsewhere: it keeps the robot leveled and keeps operating in the regime where the map, and thus every map-based baseline, breaks down, using sensing that is always available and never drifts.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\nopagebreak
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We identify and quantify a dependency shared by state-of-the-art flipper controllers: a requirement for an elevation map of the ground \emph{under} the robot, which is occluded from onboard sensing and can only be maintained by odometry integration, whose drift degrades it in the field. 
  We measure this degradation by evaluating map-based baselines under both a \emph{ground-truth} map and the \emph{onboard-estimated} map.
  \item We propose \SAS, a mapless, localization-free flipper controller that uses
  only drift-free proprioception (gravity-referenced IMU attitude plus a
  short-horizon motion cue), combining continuous roll+pitch stabilization with
  reactive anti-stuck recovery under a directional-priority blend.
  \item We deploy \SAS{} on a real four-track robot (MARV) and evaluate it on real
  obstacles against baselines at three levels of autonomy: \emph{manual}
  teleoperation~\cite{larochelle2013,cihala2025}, \emph{semi-autonomous} switching between
  reactive experts~\cite{cihala2025,zimmermann2014}, and \emph{autonomous} map-based
  controllers~\cite{azayev2022,chen2023,pan2023atd3qn,xu2024,ctrac2025}, using cognitive
  load, success rate, and traversal quality (acceleration/shock, tilt, and ground clearance).
  \item We give an honest account of its limitations (residual oscillation and
  lower peak traversal quality) and characterize the operating regime in which
  mapless control is the preferable choice.
\end{enumerate}
