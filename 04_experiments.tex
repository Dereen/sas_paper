\subsection{Platform and onboard pipeline}
We evaluate the MARV robot in Gazebo simulation~\cite{cihala2025}. The onboard perception pipeline that the
map-based baselines consume is representative of current practice: an Ouster LiDAR
and IMU feed point-cloud deskewing and LiDAR-ICP odometry, which drive
elevation mapping~\cite{fankhauser2018,loam2014,pomerleau2013icp} to produce the
robot-centric elevation map. \SAS{} bypasses this pipeline entirely and reads only the
IMU and the motion cue. In every run an experienced operator controls the robot's
heading and forward velocity; only the flippers are under test, and every command
the operator issues is logged for the cognitive-load metric.

\subsection{Ground-truth map for a fair baseline}
The onboard map is exactly what we claim is the weak link, so judging the map-based
baselines only on a broken map would be unfair. We therefore run each of them twice:
once with the \emph{onboard-estimated} map, and once with a \emph{ground-truth} map
that removes odometry drift. In simulation the ground-truth map is read directly from the
simulator, while the onboard-estimated map is produced by running the same LiDAR-ICP
odometry and elevation-mapping pipeline on the simulated sensor streams, so it accumulates
exactly the drift we study. The gap between a baseline's two scores measures its
sensitivity to onboard mapping; \SAS{} has a single score because it uses no map.

\subsection{Obstacles}
We evaluate in the Gazebo warehouse arena of our previous work~\cite{cihala2025}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:obstacles}): a straight aisle lined with \emph{twelve} numbered obstacles of
increasing difficulty, including pallets, stacked boxes, planks, a staircase, and loose
debris. The course is reproduced unchanged from~\cite{cihala2025}, so the results are
directly comparable to the controllers evaluated there. The planks, loose debris, and
staircase are where onboard mapping is hardest (occlusion, slip, abrupt pitch) and where
anti-stuck recovery matters most.

\subsection{Baselines}
We compare against, in increasing autonomy: (i) \textbf{Manual control}, an
expert operator driving all four flippers by hand (the traversal-quality
ceiling~\cite{cihala2025}), and \textbf{Manual modes}, the operator switching between
preset flipper modes by hand~\cite{azayev2022}; (ii) the \textbf{semi-autonomous \GC/\OA{} policy}~\cite{cihala2025},
in which the operator switches between two map-based experts: ground-clearance (\GC), a
\emph{reactive} controller that regulates chassis clearance from the local terrain height,
and obstacle-aware (\OA), a \emph{geometric} controller that plans flipper poses from the
obstacle geometry ahead; (iii) \textbf{Stabilization-only}, attitude
stabilization without anti-stuck; (iv) \textbf{AntiStuck-only}, the anti-stuck dig
recovery without stabilization~\cite{azayev2022}; (v) every map-based
controller catalogued in Table~\ref{tab:sensing}, reimplemented as in
Sec.~\ref{sec:related} and spanning the geometric, hierarchical, and RL families; and (vi)
our \textbf{\SAS}. Every map-based baseline is run under both the ground-truth and the
onboard map. The stabilization-only and anti-stuck-only variants are ablations
of \SAS{} itself, isolating the contribution of, respectively, its stabilization and its
anti-stuck component.

\paragraph{Implementation provenance.} Where the authors released code we run their
implementation rather than a guess: the configuration-space
planner~\cite{yuan2019,yuan2019code}, Azayev and Zimmermann's state
machine~\cite{azayev2022,azayev2022code}, Mitriakov et al.'s RL
framework~\cite{mitriakov2021,mitriakov2022,mitriakov2022code}, and Oehler and von
Stryk's pose predictor~\cite{oehler2024,oehler2024code}, with the FTR-Bench
suite~\cite{ftrbench2025,ftrbench2025code} vendored as our RL substrate. The remaining
baselines are closed-source; for each we build a \emph{best-effort reimplementation}
from the paper, faithful to every detail it specifies: Chen's GFMP~\cite{chen2023}, Xu's
HTO~\cite{xu2024}, Zhang's planner~\cite{zhang2025}, the D3QN
variants~\cite{pan2023atd3qn,pan2023icm}, C-TRAC~\cite{ctrac2025}, and Pecka et
al.~\cite{pecka2016}.

\subsection{Metrics}
Following the Quality-Load framework~\cite{cihala2025} we report three axes.
\textbf{Success rate} (SR): fraction of runs that traverse the obstacle.
\textbf{Cognitive load} (CL): the operator's teleoperation-command count over the
obstacle, normalized as in~\cite{cihala2025,labonte2010} (lower is better).
\textbf{Traversal quality} (TQ): the mean of three normalized sub-metrics: (a)
\emph{acceleration/shock} $s$ from the IMU, (b) body \emph{tilt} (roll/pitch
magnitude), and (c) \emph{ground clearance} $d$ of the chassis. Failed runs are penalized
$\mathrm{SR}{=}0$, $\mathrm{TQ}{=}0$, $\mathrm{CL}{=}1$.

\subsection{Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports SR, CL, and TQ per method under both a
ground-truth and an onboard map; the gap between the two halves isolates the effect of
the map. The expected finding, consistent with our observations, is that map-based
baselines are strong under a ground-truth map but degrade sharply under the onboard map on
the cluttered, stepped obstacles, while \SAS{} is unaffected and attains the lowest tilt
and a competitive success rate at a low cognitive load, at the cost of lower peak quality
and residual oscillation.
