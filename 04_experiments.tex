% ============================ EXPERIMENTS ============================
\subsection{Platform and onboard pipeline}
The experimental robot is an articulated \emph{four-track} robot whose four independently actuated flippers are also its only
driven tracks (Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}). The robot software backbone is ROS 2, which allows for the development of a distributed robotic system. We evaluate the flipper controllers both in simulation and on the
real platform. The onboard perception pipeline that the map-based baselines
consume is representative of current practice: an Ouster LiDAR and IMU feed point-cloud
deskewing and LiDAR-ICP odometry~\cite{norlabicpmapper,libpointmatcher}, which drive elevation
mapping~\cite{fankhauser2018} to produce a robot-centric
elevation map, which we call the \emph{onboard map}.

\paragraph{Simulation comparison} We simulate in Gazebo Ionic~\cite{gazebo2004} with the DART physics engine. Unlike
typical simulators that approximate tracks with multiple wheels, we employ the track
simulation of~\cite{pecka2017tracks}, which models conveyor-belt behavior. The sensors match the real payload: a simulated Ouster OS0
($1024\times64$ rays at \SI{10}{\hertz}, \SIrange{0.1}{50}{\metre}, \SI{1}{\centi\metre}
Gaussian range noise) and a \SI{50}{\hertz} IMU with Gaussian noise and a slowly varying
bias. The onboard-map condition runs the robot's ICP and elevation-mapping nodes on these
simulated ROS 2 topics. Ground-truth pose and map for evaluation are taken from the
simulator. The arena is a twelve-obstacle warehouse course (Fig.~\ref{fig:obstacles}): a straight aisle lined with twelve numbered obstacles of
variable difficulty, including pallets, stacked boxes, planks, and a staircase.

\paragraph{Real robot comparison} The real arena repeats the simulation experiment on one pallet obstacle between
two reset zones (Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}). AprilTag markers on the robot provide absolute localization in the handcrafted
ground-truth map.

\paragraph{Autonomous run and \SAS{} override} In all reported controller runs, the flippers are driven autonomously, ensuring that the comparison is not biased by an operator's control; in the two manual references, which set the reference level, the operator drives the flippers by hand from a third-person view. A drive node drives the robot in every run at a constant
\SI{0.6}{\metre\per\second} in simulation and, for safety, \SI{0.25}{\metre\per\second}
on the real robot. A controller that stalls on an obstacle is released by the \emph{\SAS{} override}, which
hands the flippers to \SAS{} anti-stuck dig mode until the robot moves again.

\subsection{Baselines}
We evaluate \SAS{} together with two manual references: \textbf{Manual control}, an expert operator driving all four flippers by hand from a
third-person view of the robot, and \textbf{Manual modes}, the same operator switching between preset flipper modes by hand~\cite{azayev2022}, and with every map-based controller cataloged in Table~\ref{tab:sensing}. 

Where the authors released code, we run their implementation; where they did not, we use our reimplementation from the paper.

The geometry-based controllers were adapted to the four-track morphology as follows. Chen's
GFMP~\cite{chen2023} keeps the exact two-contact solver and waypoint dynamic programming but admits only flipper-supported contact cases,
since the chassis is passive, adds an absolute-pitch levelness cost, and uses the
four-track morphology and the onboard-map profile. Yuan's configuration-space
planner~\cite{yuan2019} was reimplemented for the four-track morphology; it estimates the
step distance and height at every control step from the onboard map, replans the flipper trajectory continuously, and holds the flippers where its model is undefined. Oehler and von Stryk's pose predictor~\cite{oehler2024}, given a four-flipper model with a passive chassis, gains the selection stage it lacks: a
43-candidate sweep commanding the highest-stability-margin configuration, holding when
no configuration is stable. Xu's HTO~\cite{xu2024} assumes a driven chassis that carries the step edge; with a passive chassis, the chassis-contact regimes of its hybrid model are replaced on ascents by a double-prop
flipper bridge with enforced chassis clearance, plus rear-release and
descent-preparation schedules. Zhang's planner~\cite{zhang2025} is reproduced only as its
contact-model pose prediction and action cost, solved at a virtual station
\SI{0.5}{\metre} ahead; chassis-supported poses are forbidden on the passive chassis. The mapless posture controller of Rocha
et al.~\cite{rocha2023} is not evaluated, since it is not designed for obstacle traversal.

The learned controllers were adapted as follows. Pan's
AT-D3QN~\cite{pan2023atd3qn}: the flipper reward $R_{\text{flipper}}$ is mirrored onto the
rear flippers, and the target pitch is referenced to the ground rather than to the chassis,
since on a four-track robot the rear flippers are part of the drive train and the ride
height is a free variable; the contact reward $R_{\text{contact}}$ of ICM-D3QN is added and
redefined around flipper-pair grounding and chassis clearance. Pan's
ICM-D3QN~\cite{pan2023icm}: the same three changes, sharing the code with AT-D3QN;
otherwise, as published. C-TRAC~\cite{ctrac2025}: reward terms are added that incentivize
movement and prevent convergence to a fixed pose; trained at a fixed forward velocity.
Mitriakov~\cite{mitriakov2021}: the 2-DoF arm is dropped, so the center-of-gravity penalty
reduces to a chassis-tilt proxy; one policy covers both driving directions, and the rule
that the trailing flipper never goes below neutral is inverted, since on a four-track robot
that flipper carries a load; trained at a fixed forward velocity. Pecka~\cite{pecka2016}: the
sign convention follows the authors' reference implementation rather than the paper, and
safety is read from the same rollout; the real-robot training stage is omitted.

\subsection{Ground-truth map for a fair baseline}
The map-based baselines depend on the onboard map, so scoring them under that map alone
would mix the quality of the controller with the quality of its perception. We therefore
run each of them twice: with the onboard map and localization, and with a
\emph{ground-truth} map that is free of odometry noise. The difference between the two scores is the baseline's
sensitivity to onboard mapping.

\subsection{Metrics}
Following~\cite{cihala2025} we report \textbf{traversal quality} (TQ), the mean of three
normalized and distance-binned channel scores of a traversal, shock $q_s$, body tilt
$q_t$, and ground clearance $q_c$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TQ}=\tfrac{1}{3}\,(q_s+q_t+q_c)\in(0,1].
  \label{eq:tq}
\end{equation}
We also report the percentage of the obstacle distance for which the \SAS{} override dig drove the flippers.
Traversal success and traversal time are not informative in this setting: the override
releases every stall, so every obstacle is completed, and the drive node drives at a constant speed; therefore, the traversal time differs between controllers only by the time spent in the stall state before the override activates.
\label{sec:metrics-block}%

\noindent\textbf{Shock} measures the impacts transmitted to the body, which stress
the robot and its payload. It is the acceleration magnitude $s=|a|$ from the IMU, normalized with the scale $s_{\max}$, the
largest acceleration the platform records. On the real robot $s_{\max}$ is the highest measured
sample of the comparison, \SI{57.5}{\metre\per\second\squared}. In simulation, the rigid contacts of DART produce single-step spikes, so $s_{\max}$ is the highest measured \SI{0.1}{\second} mean of
$s$ over all runs, \SI{376}{\metre\per\second\squared}.

\noindent\textbf{Tilt} measures how far the body departs from the gravity-aligned
horizontal; it is what the attitude stabilization regulates, and it measures how safely the controller rides. It is the angle
$t$ between the body $z$ axis and the vertical, from a level pose, normalized with
$t_{\max}=\ang{50}$.

\noindent\textbf{Ground clearance} measures whether the chassis is kept off the terrain. We measure it as the
closest ground point of a $10\times8$ grid with \SI{5}{\centi\metre}
cells under the chassis, covering its $50\times\SI{40}{\centi\metre}$ footprint, read from the ground-truth map. The four-track morphology leaves a \SI{7.3}{\centi\metre} gap under the chassis on flat
ground with level flippers, which the score takes as its reference. Rewarding raw clearance would favor
baselines that freeze their flippers and coast in a high pose.

\noindent\textbf{Normalization.} The three channels are physical quantities in different
units, and each has to become a score in $(0,1]$ where $1$ is the best, so that they can be
averaged in~\eqref{eq:tq} and compared across controllers. Shock and tilt are unbounded above, so a non-negative sample $x$ is
mapped by the sigmoid of~\cite{cihala2025},
\begin{equation}
  N(x,\hat{x}) = 2 - \frac{2}{1 + e^{-x/\hat{x}}},
  \label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
against a reference $\hat{x}$: $N=1$ at $x=0$, $N\approx0.54$ when the sample reaches
$\hat{x}$, and $N\to0$ for large samples. Each channel is scored as $q=N(x,x_{\max}/2)$ with
its own scale $x_{\max}$, so the score is close to linear for small samples and saturates
smoothly; a single extreme sample cannot dominate the average. Ground clearance is bounded by the chassis itself and uses the ramp above instead.

\noindent\textbf{Distance binning} is used rather than time binning 
because each controller takes a different amount of time to traverse the same obstacle. The measured data are divided into bins of \SI{0.25}{\metre}
along the driving direction of an obstacle; the worst per-sample score in each bin is kept, and the
channel score is the mean of these bin values. 

Averaging the three channels in~\eqref{eq:tq} assumes that they carry different
information, so they should not correlate strongly; their pairwise Spearman correlations
over the traversed obstacles (Table~\ref{tab:spearman}) are at most $0.39$, which suggests that none of our evaluation channels are redundant.

\subsection{Simulation results}
Table~\ref{tab:results} reports TQ and its three channels per controller in simulation under
the ground-truth and the onboard map, and the percentage of obstacle distance the \SAS{}
override held each controller. The manual rides remain the reference: manual control
scores $0.89$, $0.05$ above the best controller, and the difference lies almost entirely in
the clearance channel ($0.87$ against $0.61$--$0.75$ for the controllers), while shock and tilt are within $0.03$
of the operator for the best controllers. Every controller loses quality under the onboard
map, by $0.008$ to $0.069$. \SAS{} loses $0.020$, through its LiDAR-ICP motion cue alone.
Most controllers required the override for 7\% to 15\% of the obstacle distance under
either map to finish the obstacles. The shock channel hardly separates the controllers,
$0.86$ to $0.92$: all of them experienced a similar acceleration, dominated by the rigid
contacts of the simulator rather than by the flipper motion. C-TRAC degrades the most under the onboard map, by $0.069$, and shows the highest
sensitivity to the elevated noise of the onboard map: never overridden under the
ground-truth map, it is held for 7.5\% of the distance under the onboard map. For Yuan, Oehler, and
Mitriakov, the override share dropped under the onboard map: the added noise made them move their flippers, which resulted in successful traversals.

\subsection{Real-robot results}
On the real pallet (Table~\ref{tab:results}) the operator remains the reference: both manual
rides score $0.81$, above every controller. Three findings stand out. First, the best
controllers are Yuan under the onboard map ($0.72$) and Oehler and Mitriakov under the
ground-truth map (both $0.71$). Second, \SAS{} is among the best controllers on the real
robot as well, second under the onboard map behind Yuan. Third, stalls are rarer than in simulation: the map-based controllers that stall
are released by the \SAS{} override for at most 7.1\% of the crossing distance, and, unlike in simulation, the onboard map lowers that share for most of them; the pallet is also easier to
mount on the real robot, whose track flaps catch the edge. The shock channel again has a small spread, $0.76$ to
$0.80$, so the controllers experienced a similar acceleration on the pallet as well. The two map
conditions separate the controllers far less here than in simulation because one crossing is short and
the perception stack is reset before each of them, so the onboard map has little time to drift.

\subsection{Hidden obstacle}
Figure~\ref{fig:grass} shows a real experiment with a log hidden in tall grass, with Yuan and with \SAS. The LiDAR does not penetrate the grass, which causes the onboard map to show the grass
canopy, and the log is absent from it. Yuan interprets the gap that the log creates in the grass as a drop and lowers the front
flippers to descend; the lowered flippers slide under the log, causing the robot to get stuck underneath it. \SAS{} does not rely on the map, keeps its default posture, and crosses the log.

\subsection{Onboard-map error}
Figure~\ref{fig:maperr} shows how the onboard map degrades over an obstacle, in simulation
and on the real pallet. The onboard map is compared with the ground-truth map over the
body footprint (the chassis and \SI{1.5}{\metre} ahead, at a grid resolution of
\SI{5}{\centi\metre}) at every
map update, every \SI{0.25}{\second}. The error grows while the robot ascends the obstacle,
where the ground under the body was last seen from the flat approach, and falls again on
the descent, where the ground ahead comes back into view. On the real robot the error peaks five
times higher than in simulation, and the share of cells off by more than \SI{10}{\centi\metre}
fifteen times higher, so the onboard map is not merely shifted but far noisier.
