\subsection{Platform and onboard pipeline}
We evaluate the MARV robot both in Gazebo simulation~\cite{cihala2025,gazebo2004} and
on the real platform. The onboard perception pipeline that the map-based baselines
consume is representative of current practice: an Ouster LiDAR and IMU feed point-cloud
deskewing and LiDAR-ICP odometry, which drive elevation
mapping~\cite{fankhauser2018,loam2014,pomerleau2013icp} to produce the robot-centric
elevation map.

\paragraph{Simulation} Unlike typical simulators that approximate tracks with multiple
wheels, we employ a track simulation that models conveyor-belt behavior, and run it at a
real-time factor of one~\cite{cihala2025}. The sensors match the real payload: a
simulated Ouster OS0 ($1024\times64$ rays at \SI{10}{\hertz}, \SIrange{0.1}{50}{\metre},
\SI{1}{\centi\metre} Gaussian range noise) and a \SI{50}{\hertz} IMU with Gaussian noise
and a slowly varying bias. The onboard-map condition runs the same ICP and
elevation-mapping stack as the real robot on these noisy streams; the ground-truth
condition reads pose and map from the simulator.

\paragraph{Automated ride} In the sweeps reported here nobody is on the sticks. A ride
node drives the robot at a constant \SI{0.6}{\metre\per\second} with a proportional
heading regulator aimed at the start of the next obstacle field, so heading and speed are
identical in every run and both map conditions; only the flippers differ. A \SI{1}{\metre}
neutral strip separates consecutive fields; on entering it the flippers are flattened and
the robot is placed at the start of the following field, so every obstacle is approached
from the same pose. A controller that stalls inside a field is released by a stuck
watchdog that hands the flippers to \SAS{} until the robot moves again, and the field is
finished; these interventions are counted and reported. A field not left within
\SI{120}{\second} is scored as not traversed. The operator-driven reference rides are
separate and use the same course.

\subsection{Ground-truth map for a fair baseline}
Our claim is that the onboard map is the limiting input, so evaluating the map-based
baselines under that map alone would conflate the controller with its perception. We
therefore run each of them twice:
once with the \emph{onboard-estimated} map, and once with a \emph{ground-truth} map
that removes odometry drift. In simulation the ground-truth map is read directly from the
simulator; on the real robot it comes from external localization and a handcrafted elevation
map of each obstacle. In both settings the onboard-estimated map is produced by running the
LiDAR-ICP odometry and elevation-mapping pipeline on the sensor streams, so it accumulates
exactly the drift we study. The gap between a baseline's two scores measures its
sensitivity to onboard mapping.

\subsection{Baselines}
We compare against, in increasing autonomy: (i) \textbf{Manual control}, an expert
operator driving all four flippers by hand (the traversal-quality
ceiling~\cite{cihala2025}), and \textbf{Manual modes}, the operator switching between
preset flipper modes by hand~\cite{azayev2022}; (ii) the \textbf{\GC/\OA{}
policy}~\cite{cihala2025} in its automated form: the rear flippers always run
ground-clearance (\GC), a \emph{reactive} controller that regulates chassis clearance
from the local terrain height, and the front flippers start in obstacle-aware (\OA), a
\emph{geometric} controller that plans flipper poses from the obstacle geometry ahead.
Where the original had the operator toggle each front flipper between the two, here a
stuck criterion identical to the watchdog's switches both fronts to \GC{} after
\SI{2}{\second} of no progress, holds them there for \SI{2}{\second}, and returns them
to \OA; (iii) every map-based controller catalogued in Table~\ref{tab:sensing}; and our
\textbf{\SAS}. Every map-based baseline is run under both the ground-truth and the
onboard map.

\paragraph{Implementation provenance.} Where the authors released code we run their
implementation; where they did not, we use our most faithful reimplementation from the
paper. Released code: the configuration-space planner~\cite{yuan2019,yuan2019code},
Azayev and Zimmermann's state machine~\cite{azayev2022,azayev2022code}, Mitriakov et
al.'s RL framework~\cite{mitriakov2021,mitriakov2022,mitriakov2022code}, and Oehler and
von Stryk's pose predictor~\cite{oehler2024,oehler2024code}, with the FTR-Bench
suite~\cite{ftrbench2025,ftrbench2025code} as our RL substrate. Reimplemented from the
paper: Chen's GFMP~\cite{chen2023}, Xu's HTO~\cite{xu2024}, Zhang's
planner~\cite{zhang2025}, the D3QN variants~\cite{pan2023atd3qn,pan2023icm},
C-TRAC~\cite{ctrac2025}, and Pecka et al.~\cite{pecka2016}. Two adaptations were needed
for a four-track robot with no belts under the chassis. Poses in which the chassis
rests on the terrain, which the six-track originals of Chen and Zhang treat as valid
support, ground MARV out, so those poses are excluded from their pose solvers. Oehler's
pose predictor is instantiated on MARV's own kinematic model over a live signed-distance
field built from the same LiDAR. All other mechanisms are left as published.

\subsection{Metrics}
Following~\cite{cihala2025} we report \textbf{traversal quality} (TQ). A success
rate is not informative here: the stuck watchdog releases every stalled controller, so
nearly every field is traversed, and the cost of stalling appears instead in the
watchdog's intervention count and in TQ. TQ is the mean of three normalized sub-metrics: (a)
\emph{acceleration/shock} $s$ from the IMU, (b) body \emph{tilt} (roll/pitch
magnitude), and (c) \emph{ground clearance}. Prior flipper control uses ground clearance as
a control target or planning constraint (Rocha et al.\ regulate it~\cite{rocha2023};
geometry planners enforce ground contact~\cite{chen2023,xu2024}); we use it instead as a
scored quality. Rather than reward maximal clearance, we score the \emph{deviation} of the
chassis clearance from its resting value on flat ground: the score peaks in a deadband around
that flat-ground clearance and falls to zero both at belly contact and far above it. Rewarding raw clearance would favor
baselines that freeze their flippers and coast in a high pose; penalizing deviation does not.
\label{sec:metrics-block}%
Each channel is reduced over a traversal by a \emph{distance-binned maximum}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:conditioning}). Every \SI{0.25}{\metre} of obstacle forms one bin; the
worst normalized sample in each bin is taken, and the bins are averaged. Two choices are
being made here, and both have precedent outside flipper control.

\emph{Why bins of a fixed length, rather than a fixed number of them.} The ICRA 2025
estimator~\cite{cihala2025} splits a traversal into ten equal shares of its
\emph{samples}, which weights the terrain by how long the robot lingered on it, so a
stall is counted once as poor quality and again as more of it. Binning by distance gives
every metre of obstacle one vote whatever it cost in seconds. The KITTI odometry metric
averages error over fixed-length path segments for the same reason~\cite{geiger2012kitti}.

\emph{Why the worst sample in a bin, rather than its mean.} The shock channel is made of
sub-second transients (Sec.~\ref{sec:conditioning}), and a mean over samples averages them
away. Taking a bin maximum and then averaging the bins is the \emph{block-maxima}
estimator of extreme-value statistics~\cite{coles2001extremes}, and the same construction
is what ISO 2631-1 prescribes for exactly this situation: when the crest factor of a
vibration signal is high, the standard replaces the plain RMS with the maximum of a running
RMS over short windows, because the RMS understates a spiky signal~\cite{iso2631}. Our
bins are cut in space rather than in time, but the estimator is the same one, and it
keeps the statistic a distribution summary rather than the single worst instant of the
ride. The closest precedent within flipper control is the spatial gating
of~\cite{chen2023}. Failed runs are penalized $\mathrm{SR}{=}0$ and $\mathrm{TQ}{=}0$.

\subsection{Signal conditioning}\label{sec:conditioning}
The raw streams are noisy in different ways, and none of them is low-pass
filtered before scoring. \emph{Shock} is the acceleration magnitude $|a|$ from
the IMU at \SI{21}{\hertz}; contact with a track lug or a step edge appears as a
transient a few samples long, and those transients are exactly what the channel exists
to see, so averaging them away would change what is measured rather than clean it. \emph{Clearance} is not read from a range sensor at all: it is a
$10\times8$ grid of points on \SI{5}{\centi\metre} spacing under the chassis,
looked up in the elevation map and reduced to the \emph{closest} ground point,
i.e.\ the worst of eighty lookups. That reduction is deliberately conservative,
and it means a single catastrophic map cell---the tail quantified in
Sec.~\ref{sec:whydegrade}---propagates straight into the channel. \emph{Tilt}
is taken from the pose rather than from the IMU's reported orientation, which
carries a fixed mounting offset that makes it unusable as an attitude directly.

Rather than smooth the signals, we bound their influence at four points:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Hole filling.} The terrain under the chassis is occluded, so the
  raw elevation layer has no data exactly where clearance is measured. The
  lookup reads the \emph{inpainted} layer of the mapping filter chain, so an
  occluded cell is interpolated rather than dropped; without it the channel
  would be undefined under the robot.
  \item \textbf{Deadband.} Deviations from the nominal ride height smaller than
  \SI{1}{\centi\metre} below it or \SI{2}{\centi\metre} above score zero
  badness. The band is asymmetric because the two directions are not equally
  dangerous: below the target the belly is close, above it is merely wasteful.
  Small disturbances are the robot working, not the robot failing.
  \item \textbf{Bounded normalizers.} Shock passes through
  $N(s,s_{\max}/2)$ with $s_{\max}=\SI{20}{\metre\per\second\squared}$, and
  clearance is clipped to $[0,1]$, saturating at belly contact below and
  \SI{10}{\centi\metre} above. No single sample can dominate a bin, whatever
  the sensor does.
  \item \textbf{Bin maximum, then mean.} The worst normalized sample in each
  spatial bin is taken and the bins averaged. Taking a rank statistic per
  bin and averaging those, rather than averaging samples, keeps a rare spike
  visible without letting the single worst instant of the ride stand for the
  whole traversal.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Results}
Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_ablation} reports TQ per controller in simulation under the
ground-truth and the onboard map. Every controller, \SAS{} included, loses quality under
the onboard map; the degradation is small, and it is caused by the noise the perception
stack adds to the observations the controllers act on. \SAS{} degrades through its motion
cue, which reads that same noise through LiDAR-ICP. In one C-TRAC run under the
ground-truth map the robot tipped on obstacle~7 (\ang{88.5} of roll, \ang{128.2} from
upright), the only tip-over in either campaign; that obstacle is scored as not traversed
with $\mathrm{TQ}{=}0$.

\subsection{Where the degradation comes from}\label{sec:whydegrade}
Fig.~\ref{fig:gt_ablation} shows every map-based controller losing quality when
it has to build its own map, but a drop alone does not say \emph{why}. The two
candidate causes---the robot not knowing where it is, and its map of the ground
being wrong---are separable in simulation, so we measured both against the
simulator's ground truth rather than asserting one.

\paragraph{Localization} We report \emph{incremental} error, how far the
estimator thinks it moved over a \SI{1}{\second} window against how far it
really moved; integrated drift is not meaningful on this course because the
ride teleports the robot between obstacles. LiDAR-ICP gives
\SI{1.4}{\centi\metre\per\second} mean error (p95 \SI{3.5}{\centi\metre\per\second}),
against \SI{5.4}{} for IMU$+$wheel fusion and \SI{23.6}{} for wheel odometry
alone. The resulting offset of the ICP \texttt{map} frame from the true world is
\SI{5.1}{\centi\metre} on average over the course and \SI{4.6}{\centi\metre} on
the obstacles, almost all of it longitudinal.

\paragraph{Perception} The arena is static, so the two conditions are compared per
\emph{physical point} rather than per run: every \texttt{grid\_map} message carries its
window pose and resolution, so each cell has a world coordinate, and cells
within \SI{2}{\metre} of the robot are accumulated into one \SI{5}{\centi\metre}
field per condition. Registering the ground-truth field against itself gives
\SI{0.42}{\centi\metre} RMSE on the obstacle bands, the noise floor of the
method; the onboard field gives \SI{2.20}{\centi\metre}.

\paragraph{Perception dominates} The onboard field is accumulated twice: once
with its cells where the robot believes they are, which is what the controller
actually plans on, and once pushed into true world by the full rigid transform
$T_{wm}=T_{wb}\,T_{mb}^{-1}$ evaluated per message, which removes the
localization error entirely and leaves perception alone. The difference between
the two is the localization contribution, and it is small: RMSE moves from
\SI{2.20}{} to \SI{2.18}{\centi\metre} on the obstacles ($2.1\%$ of the squared
error) and from \SI{2.83}{} to \SI{2.64}{\centi\metre} over the whole course
($13.3\%$). The \SI{5}{\centi\metre} ICP offset is real but nearly irrelevant to
what the controller reads; the map is wrong mostly because it was \emph{seen}
wrong, not because it was \emph{placed} wrong.

\paragraph{The error's shape predicts the failure mode} The onboard map is not
uniformly noisy. Its median error on the obstacles is \SI{0.71}{\centi\metre},
essentially perfect, while p99 is \SI{9.1}{\centi\metre} and $0.8\%$ of cells
are wrong by more than \SI{10}{\centi\metre}. Against a nominal chassis ride
height of \SI{7.0}{\centi\metre}, an error that large is not degraded vision: it
is a step that is not there, or one that is missing. A uniformly noisy map would
blunt quality everywhere; rare catastrophic cells instead make a controller
occasionally commit to the wrong flipper pose and stall. \emph{The cost of the
onboard map is paid in stalls, not in roughness.}

\paragraph{The defect is in the approach, not on the obstacle} Counter to the
obvious intuition, the flat gaps between obstacles are mapped about twice as
badly as the obstacles themselves (RMSE \SI{4.35}{} against
\SI{2.20}{\centi\metre}, and $3.8\%$ against $0.8\%$ of cells wrong by more than
\SI{10}{\centi\metre}). The cause is geometric: the LiDAR sits high and sees a
nearby obstacle at a favourable incidence angle, while flat ground further ahead
is grazed, where a small range error projects into a large height error and the
robot's own shadow is a large fraction of what it is trying to measure. A
controller that reads a false step \emph{during the approach} therefore sets the
wrong flipper pose before it arrives, and has to recover on the obstacle.

\paragraph{\SAS{} is not exempt} \SAS{} loses $0.043$ across the two conditions, in
the middle of the field, and it is worth being explicit about why a controller
that reads no map moves at all. Its stabilization term is map-free and
gravity-referenced, and does not change between the conditions; its motion cue $m$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method-antistuck}) is the short-window variance of LiDAR-ICP
odometry, which carries the perception stack's noise into the stuck detection.
