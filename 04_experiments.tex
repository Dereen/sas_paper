\subsection{Platform and onboard pipeline}
The robot is a tracked robot with four independently actuated flippers
(Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}). We evaluate the flipper controllers both in simulation and on the
real platform. The onboard perception pipeline that the map-based baselines
consume is representative of current practice: an Ouster LiDAR and IMU feed point-cloud
deskewing and LiDAR-ICP odometry~\cite{norlabicpmapper,libpointmatcher}, which drive elevation
mapping~\cite{fankhauser2018} to produce a robot-centric
elevation map, which we call the \emph{onboard map}.

\paragraph{Simulation comparison} We simulate in Gazebo~\cite{gazebo2004}. Unlike
typical simulators that approximate tracks with multiple wheels, we employ the track
simulation of~\cite{pecka2017tracks}, which models conveyor-belt behavior. The sensors match the real payload: a simulated Ouster OS0
($1024\times64$ rays at \SI{10}{\hertz}, \SIrange{0.1}{50}{\metre}, \SI{1}{\centi\metre}
Gaussian range noise) and a \SI{50}{\hertz} IMU with Gaussian noise and a slowly varying
bias. The onboard-map condition runs the robot's ICP and elevation-mapping nodes on these
simulated streams; the ground-truth condition takes the pose and the map from the
simulator. The arena is a twelve-obstacle warehouse course (Fig.~\ref{fig:obstacles}): a straight aisle lined with twelve numbered obstacles of
variable difficulty, including pallets, stacked boxes, planks, and a staircase.

\paragraph{Real robot comparison} The real arena repeats the simulation experiment on one pallet obstacle between
two reset zones (Fig.~\ref{fig:robot}). AprilTag markers on the robot and in the arena provide absolute localization that registers
the handcrafted ground-truth map.

\paragraph{Autonomous ride and \SAS{} override} In all reported runs the robot is
driven autonomously, so the comparison is not biased by an operator's control. A drive node
drives the robot at a constant
\SI{0.6}{\metre\per\second} in simulation and, for safety, \SI{0.25}{\metre\per\second}
on the real robot. A controller that stalls on an obstacle is released by the \emph{\SAS{} override}, which
hands the flippers to \SAS{} until the robot moves again, and the obstacle is finished. An
obstacle that is not left within \SI{120}{\second} counts as not traversed, and a controller
that fails to traverse an obstacle is scored zero on it.

\subsection{Baselines}
We evaluate \SAS{} together with two manual references, \textbf{Manual control}, an expert operator driving all four flippers by hand from a
third-person view of the robot, and \textbf{Manual modes}, the same operator switching
between preset flipper modes by hand~\cite{azayev2022}, and with every map-based
controller catalogued in Table~\ref{tab:sensing}. Where the authors released code we run their
implementation; where they did not, we use our reimplementation from the paper. The geometry-based
controllers were adapted to the four-track morphology as follows. Chen's
GFMP~\cite{chen2023} keeps the exact two-contact solver and waypoint dynamic programming, but admits only flipper-supported contact cases,
since the chassis is passive, adds an absolute-pitch levelness cost, and uses the
four-track morphology and the onboard-map profile. Yuan's configuration-space
planner~\cite{yuan2019} was reimplemented for the four-track morphology; it estimates the
step distance and height at every control step from the onboard map instead of motion
capture, replans continuously, and holds the flippers where its model is undefined. Oehler and von Stryk's pose predictor~\cite{oehler2024}, given a four-flipper model with a passive chassis, gains the selection stage it lacks: a
43-candidate sweep commanding the highest-stability-margin configuration, holding when
no configuration is stable. Xu's HTO~\cite{xu2024} assumes a driven chassis that carries the step edge; with a passive chassis the
chassis-contact regimes of its hybrid model are replaced on ascents by a double-prop
flipper bridge with enforced chassis clearance, plus rear-release and
descent-preparation schedules. Zhang's planner~\cite{zhang2025} is reproduced only as its
contact-model pose prediction and action cost, solved at a virtual station
\SI{0.5}{\metre} ahead; chassis-supported poses are forbidden on the passive chassis. The learned controllers were adapted as follows. Pan's
AT-D3QN~\cite{pan2023atd3qn}: the flipper reward $R_{\text{flipper}}$ is mirrored onto the
rear flippers and the target pitch is referenced to the ground rather than to the chassis,
since on a four-track robot the rear flippers are part of the drivetrain and the ride
height is a free variable; the contact reward $R_{\text{contact}}$ of ICM-D3QN is added and
redefined around flipper-pair grounding and chassis clearance. Pan's
ICM-D3QN~\cite{pan2023icm}: the same three changes, sharing the code with AT-D3QN;
otherwise as published. C-TRAC~\cite{ctrac2025}: reward terms are added that incentivize
movement and prevent convergence to a fixed pose; trained at a fixed forward velocity.
Mitriakov~\cite{mitriakov2021}: the 2-DoF arm is dropped, so the center-of-gravity penalty
reduces to a chassis-tilt proxy; one policy covers both driving directions, and the rule
that the trailing flipper never goes below neutral is inverted, since on a four-track robot
that flipper carries load; trained at a fixed forward velocity. Pecka~\cite{pecka2016}: the
sign convention follows the authors' reference implementation rather than the paper, and
safety is read from the same rollout; the real-robot training stage is omitted.
Azayev~\cite{azayev2022}: faithful, with human gamepad demonstrations, a supervised
classifier and a fixed decoder deployed on the real stack; it deviates only in the
heightmap observation and in a hard legality-masked state instead of the soft belief.

\subsection{Ground-truth map for a fair baseline}
The map-based baselines depend on the onboard map, so scoring them under that map alone
would mix the quality of the controller with the quality of its perception. We therefore
run each of them twice: with the onboard map and localization, and with a
\emph{ground-truth} map that is free of odometry drift. The difference between the two scores is the baseline's
sensitivity to onboard mapping.

\subsection{Metrics}
Following~\cite{cihala2025} we report \textbf{traversal quality} (TQ), the mean of three
normalized channel scores of a traversal, shock $q_s$, body tilt $q_t$, and ground
clearance $q_c$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{TQ}=\tfrac{1}{3}\,(q_s+q_t+q_c).
  \label{eq:tq}
\end{equation}
\label{sec:metrics-block}%

\noindent\textbf{Normalization.} A sample of a non-negative quantity $x$ is scored as
$q=1-N(x,x_{\max}/2)$, where $N$ from~\cite{cihala2025} rises from $0$ at $x=0$ toward $1$
and reaches $0.46$ at half its scale $x_{\max}$. $N$ is close to linear for small $x$ and
saturates smoothly toward $1$, so small differences remain comparable while extreme samples
do not dominate the score.

\noindent\textbf{Shock.} Shock measures the impacts transmitted to the body, which stress
the robot and its payload. It is the acceleration magnitude $s=|a|$ from the IMU, scored
with the scale $s_{\max}$, the largest acceleration the platform records. On the real robot
$s_{\max}$ is the highest sample of the campaign, \SI{57.5}{\metre\per\second\squared}. In
Gazebo the rigid contacts of DART produce single-step spikes, so $s_{\max}$ is the highest
\SI{0.1}{\second} mean of $s$ over all runs, \SI{376}{\metre\per\second\squared}.

\noindent\textbf{Tilt.} Tilt measures how far the body departs from level; it is what the
attitude stabilization regulates, and it measures how safely the policy rides. It is the angle
$t$ between the body $z$ axis and the vertical, from the ground-truth pose, scored with
$t_{\max}=\ang{50}$.

\noindent\textbf{Ground clearance.} Clearance measures whether the chassis is kept off the terrain. We measure it as the
closest ground point of a $10\times8$ grid with \SI{5}{\centi\metre}
cells under the chassis, read from the ground-truth map. The score penalizes the deviation
of the chassis clearance from its flat-ground value outside a dead-band, linearly up to
chassis contact and \SI{10}{\centi\metre} above. Rewarding raw clearance would favor
baselines that freeze their flippers and coast in a high pose.

\noindent\textbf{Binning.} Each channel score of an obstacle is a \emph{distance-binned
maximum} of the per-sample scores: the obstacle is divided into bins of \SI{0.25}{\metre}
along the driving direction, the worst per-sample score in each bin is kept, and the
channel score is the mean of these bin values. We bin by distance rather than by time
because each controller takes a different time to traverse the same obstacle.

Averaging the three channels in~\eqref{eq:tq} assumes that they carry different
information, so they should not correlate strongly; their pairwise Spearman correlations
over the traversed obstacles (Table~\ref{tab:spearman}) are at most $0.39$.

\subsection{Simulation results}
Table~\ref{tab:results} reports TQ and its three channels per controller in Gazebo under
the ground-truth and the onboard map, and the share of obstacle distance the \SAS{}
override held each controller. The manual rides remain the reference: manual control
scores $0.89$, $0.05$ above the best controller, and the difference lies almost entirely in
the clearance channel ($0.87$ against $0.61$--$0.75$), while shock and tilt are within $0.03$ of
the operator for the best controllers. Every controller loses quality under the onboard
map, by $0.01$ to $0.11$. \SAS{} loses $0.020$, through its LiDAR-ICP motion cue alone.
The policies overall required the override for 7 to 14\% of the obstacle distance under
either map to finish the obstacles. C-TRAC degrades the most under the onboard map, by $0.11$, and shows the highest
sensitivity to the elevated noise of the onboard map: never overridden under the
ground-truth map, it is held for 7.5\% of the distance under the onboard map; in one C-TRAC
run under the ground-truth map the robot also tipped over on obstacle~7, the only tip-over
in either campaign, and that obstacle is scored as not traversed. For Yuan, Oehler and
Mitriakov the override share dropped under the onboard map: the added noise helped them
not to collapse to a fixed flipper position.

\subsection{Real-robot results}
On the real pallet (Table~\ref{tab:results}) the operator remains the reference: manual
control and manual modes both score $0.81$, above every controller. Among the
controllers, Yuan and \SAS{} lead under the onboard map at $0.70$--$0.72$, Mitriakov and Yuan
under the ground-truth map at $0.71$, and with one ride per condition the difference between
the two maps is within the spread over crossings for most of them. \SAS{} recovers from every stall by its own anti-stuck term and completes
every crossing; the map-based controllers that stall are released
by the \SAS{} override for up to 7\% of the crossing distance, and C-TRAC and ICM-D3QN
abort crossings under either map. The largest degradations under the onboard map are
ICM-D3QN ($-0.10$) and Mitriakov ($-0.08$). Unlike in Gazebo, the noise of the onboard map
lowers the override share of most controllers on the real robot: C-TRAC from 7.1 to 3.4\%,
AT-D3QN from 3.5 to 0.4\%, Oehler from 2.8 to 1.5\%, and Chen, Yuan, Xu and Zhang slightly;
only ICM-D3QN and Mitriakov need more. The tilt channel, the target of
the stabilization term, is where \SAS{} leads: $0.86$ in Gazebo, level with Chen and ahead of
the rest ($0.75$--$0.85$), and on the real pallet its maximum tilt over the ride is
\ang{11.5}, against \ang{12} to \ang{26} for the other controllers and \ang{36} for C-TRAC.

\noindent\textbf{Hidden obstacle.} Figure~\ref{fig:grass} shows an additional real
experiment, a log hidden in tall grass, with Yuan, the best map-based policy on the pallet,
and with \SAS. The LiDAR does not penetrate the grass, so the onboard map shows the grass
canopy and the log is absent from it. Yuan reads the gap the log opens in the grass as a
drop, lowers the flippers to descend, and drives itself under the log, where it gets
stuck. \SAS{} receives no such information, keeps its default posture, and crosses the log.

\subsection{Onboard-map error}
Sampled over the body footprint (the chassis and \SI{1.5}{\metre} ahead, at
\SI{5}{\centi\metre}) every \SI{0.25}{\second}, the height difference between the onboard
and the ground-truth map has a median absolute value of \SI{0.9}{\centi\metre} and a median
per-snapshot RMSE of \SI{2.6}{\centi\metre}, with 4\% of the cells off by more than
\SI{10}{\centi\metre}.
The onboard map also reports its own uncertainty, and in Gazebo it grows over every
obstacle: the median bound width of the cells under the body rises from
\SI{2.4}{\centi\metre} at the reset to \SIrange{4.5}{4.9}{\centi\metre} after
\SI{8}{\second} on the obstacle, and its 90th percentile from \SI{3}{\centi\metre} to
\SIrange{17}{20}{\centi\metre}, the occluded cells the controllers regulate on. On the real
robot the mapper's per-cell variance shrinks with fusion, from \SI{1}{\centi\metre} to
\SI{3}{\milli\metre}, and does not reflect the map error.
