\begin{abstract}
Mobile robots for rough terrain come in three kinds: legged, wheeled, and tracked.
Legged robots clear the widest range of obstacles but must actively drive their motors
just to stay upright, which gives them a low payload-to-power-consumption ratio; wheeled and tracked
robots stay upright passively and carry far more for the same power, yet neither can
climb an obstacle taller than its leading wheel. \emph{Articulated} tracked robots bridge the gap: four
independently actuated flippers give them the extra reach to clamber over steps, logs,
and rubble. Nearly every autonomous or semi-autonomous flipper controller assumes access
to an \emph{elevation map} of the ground under and around the robot. Because the terrain
beneath the chassis is occluded from the robot's exteroceptive sensors, this map cannot
be measured directly; it must be accumulated from earlier observations and carried
beneath the robot by integrating odometry, whose drift corrupts it. We present
\emph{Stabilized Anti-Stuck} (\SAS),
a mapless, localization-free controller that stabilizes body roll and pitch and frees
itself when stuck, using only proprioception. We deploy \SAS{} on our four-track robot
MARV and evaluate it on real obstacles against manual teleoperation, prior expert
flipper controllers. \SAS{} does not reach the traversal quality of methods that rely on a known map,
and its anti-stuck term can still produce oscillatory dig-release behavior; its value is that
it keeps the robot leveled and remains operational on terrain with obstacles hidden from
its sensors, such as a rigid log buried in tall grass, where map-based control breaks down. \TODO{final quantitative headline once results are in.}
\end{abstract}
